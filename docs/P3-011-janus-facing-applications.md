# Janus Facing Applications: Community Orchestration and the Red Queen Race

**Network Theory Applied Research Institute**
Document ID: P3-011 · Version: 2.1 · June 2026
*Supersedes P3-011 v1.0 (November 2025). v2.1 folds the Mycelium ledger (P3-013) and the Agrinet protocol specification (P3-012) into §3 — the record layer the v2 argument named but did not detail.*

## Abstract

The first version of this paper argued that platform power is a property of architecture rather than ownership: cooperative ownership of an extractive platform reproduces extraction, because siloed interfaces withhold the relationship-defining information that lets participants take one another's roles, and that withholding reconstructs hierarchy regardless of the cap table. That argument was right, and we keep it. What it got wrong was its destination. It treated transparency as a terminus — asymmetry gives way to disclosure, disclosure to symmetry, symmetry to democratic governance — and so mistook a static artifact for a sustained practice. Acemoglu and Robinson's narrow corridor is not a place you arrive; it is a race you run, and the balance it produces decays the instant the running stops.

This version replaces the transparent interface with *community orchestration software*: not a screen on which the same numbers are displayed to everyone, but a live coordination medium that keeps the contest between coordinating power and checking power running rather than resolving it into consensus. Properly built, such software collapses Acemoglu and Robinson's two actors — Leviathan and society — into one. But the collapse is homeostasis, not fusion: the same community continuously occupying and exchanging both roles, fast enough and legibly enough that the balance is maintained by the motion. Because no single coordination layer can sit between every actor without becoming the very Leviathan it polices, orchestration must be layered — voice where contest is cheap, exit where it is not, and a minimal, entrenched, permanently contested constitution at the one layer no one can leave. Legibility is the hinge on which the whole thing turns: it is what makes "the community is both the Leviathan and the leash" a description of self-government rather than the oldest slogan of despotism.

## 1. What the First Version Got Right — and Where It Stopped

### 1.1 Architecture over ownership

A platform's interface communicates on two levels at once. There is the content level — the literal function an element performs — and beneath it the relationship level: the metacommunication that tells a participant who they are to whom. Drawing on Watzlawick, Bavelas, and Jackson, the original paper observed that relationships come in two structures, symmetrical (between equals) and complementary (between a superior and a subordinate), and that a complementary relationship becomes pathological when it is made rigid — when the subordinate party cannot reach the level at which the relationship itself is defined.

Siloed platform interfaces metacommunicate exactly that pathology. By giving each class of participant only enough information to perform its role and not enough to see the transaction whole, they tell every participant, beneath whatever the content says, that they occupy a subordinate position and must trust calculations they cannot inspect. They also prevent what Mead called taking the role of the other: a driver cannot see what the customer sees, the customer cannot see what the driver is paid, and neither can recognize the common interest that collective action would require. Hierarchy is thereby enforced at the level of the interface, beneath ownership. This is the paper's genuine and durable contribution, and the reason cooperative ownership alone fails: ownership is downstream of architecture, and an architecture of asymmetry maintains concentrated power no matter who holds the equity.

### 1.2 The stage and the play

The error was to treat the cure as a finished state. Three gaps open the moment "transparency" is declared the destination.

Disclosure is not verification. Showing everyone the same screen is not the same as letting anyone confirm that the displayed numbers were produced honestly. The AGPL license the paper relies on secures inspectability of source code; it does not secure attestation of execution. An operator can publish an algorithm with one hand and run modified code, or feed the published algorithm selected inputs, with the other, and a beautifully transparent interface will faithfully render the manipulated result. You have been shown the recipe; you have not been let into the kitchen.

Symmetry is not an endpoint. Even where information is genuinely equal, the capacity to act on it is not. If a single vote is not pivotal and reading the financial model is costly, a rational participant declines to read it, and transparency no one consumes is decoration. Even transparent democracies drift toward rule by their most engaged minority — here the maintainers and the small governance-active subset — which is the old oligarchy in open-source clothing. And whoever can actually read the matching algorithm, and whoever controls the button that merges a change to it, holds real power regardless of what everyone else can nominally see.

Exit is expensive. The right to fork that the license guarantees does not rescue any of this, because forking a live network means leaving its liquidity behind, and a fork that costs you the whole market is not a credible threat. Exit is costly, so voice weakens, so the check atrophies — precisely the sequence Acemoglu and Robinson predict when power outruns the capacity to check it.

The first version built the stage: the lit, level surface on which everyone sees the same thing. It then called the stage the play. The play is the contest, and the contest has to go on being performed.

## 2. Community Orchestration Software

### 2.1 Two senses of orchestration

The term carries its own fork, and the entire design turns on which sense is meant. In music, orchestration means a conductor and a fixed score — a hierarchy and a static artifact, the two things the previous section warned against. In software, it means decentralized runtime coordination with no conductor at all: a service mesh, a protocol, processes coordinating through shared rules rather than central command.

A conductor-model orchestration layer sits between every actor and works to prevent collisions, prevent coercion, and maintain the "music" of the platform's activity. Each clause is a trap. "Maintain the music" is consonance — it is the town-hall consensus the narrow corridor explicitly is not, set to a soundtrack — and a layer built to prevent collisions is built to damp the very dissonance that liberty requires. Worse, "prevent coercion" smuggles sovereignty in through the side door. Preventing collisions is thin coordination, the work of a traffic signal; but *defining and stopping* coercion requires something to adjudicate what counts as coercive and to intervene, and that is a sovereign function — the Leviathan itself, hidden inside the infrastructure and answerable to no one. A coordination layer that decides what is coercive is not a neutral medium between actors. It is the despot they cannot see.

A mesh-model orchestration layer does the opposite. It does not prevent dissonance; it metabolizes it. It exposes collisions rather than suppressing them, prices friction rather than forbidding it, routes dissent rather than dissolving it, and above all makes the reversal of roles cheap and legible, so that the coordinated can become the coordinators. This is community orchestration software in the sense this paper means: not a screen, not a referee, but the live medium in which the contest runs.

### 2.2 Homeostasis, not harmony

Acemoglu and Robinson hold that liberty lives in the balance between two strong forces — a state capable enough to coordinate and a society capable enough to check it — neither permanently ahead, the running itself holding the corridor open. Faced with a genuinely self-governing community, the temptation is to declare the two forces one: the community is both the Leviathan and the leash, the people and the power are the same.

Taken literally, this does not resolve the race. It abolishes it. A race needs two runners; one runner is no race. And "we are both the power and the check on it," left unqualified, is not the corridor — it is the founding sentence of every despotism, the general will that cannot err and therefore brooks no opposition, the straight road from popular sovereignty to terror. Collapse the two and you do not enter the corridor; you leave it, into the cage of informal norms on one side, where coercion is exercised by custom and exclusion, or into concentrated authority wearing the people's name on the other.

There is, however, a reading in which the collapse is real and liberating, and it is the one this architecture is built to make true. The two forces need not be two *populations*. They can be two *functions* — coordinating and checking — that the same population carries at once and exchanges continuously. The split between state and society is in large part a historical accident: those functions landed in different bodies, and the contest between them ran slow and bloody across centuries. Community orchestration software lets a single population hold both functions and swap them quickly. The contest is not ended; it is internalized and accelerated.

The "one actor," in this reading, is the outward appearance of a rapid internal feedback loop. A thermostat looks like a single thing holding a temperature, but is internally a sensor in tension with an actuator. A murmuration looks like one organism, but is thousands of birds each running local rules. This is homeostasis, not fusion. The balance is maintained only by continuous motion and the expenditure of energy. "The system balances itself" is true in the way a living body balances itself — never finished — and false in the way a settled equilibrium is. The Red Queen still has no finish line. Software simply lets the loop run in real time instead of over generations.

What keeps the homeostatic reading honest rather than despotic is legibility. The collapse is genuine self-government only if the coordinated can in fact become the coordinators — only if anyone the orchestration layer governs can read its logic and change or fork it. An opaque layer that "maintains the music" is precisely the despotic Leviathan: a conductor no one can see, claiming to be the orchestra. A legible, forkable one lets the players seize the podium. Legibility is therefore not a feature of orchestration software; it is the condition under which the one-actor claim is true at all.

## 3. The Architecture of Orchestration

A single layer that sat between every actor would, however legible, be a single point of coordination and so a single point of capture — the conductor again, under better lighting. The resolution is to distribute the coordinating function across layers, so that almost all of it can be left and only an irreducible minimum sits at a core no one can leave. Three layers, three mechanisms: voice, exit, and a contested constitution.

### 3.1 The peer layer: contestable reputation

At the layer of individual exchange, orchestration takes the form of a reputation system, and its job is to metabolize the friction between participants rather than to adjudicate it from above. NTARI's Leveson-Based Trade Assessment Scale (LBTAS) is built for this. Borrowing from Nancy Leveson's safety engineering — where a system failure means loss of life, not a bruised ego — it treats harm as a discrete event to be surfaced, not a quantity to be smoothed.

Ratings are never averaged. A reputation is a distribution: the count of ratings received at each level, together with the total. The reason is the reason aviation does not report two crashes in seventy-three flights as a reassuring average — two crashes ground the fleet. An average buries the signal that matters; a single instance of harm, diluted by surrounding praise, becomes a comfortable number, and a comfortable number is exactly how a party who harmed someone keeps transacting. The distribution keeps every instance of harm visible. Volume is itself signal — a clean record over five thousand exchanges is a stronger trust signal than the same shape over five — but volume does not absolve harm; it sits beside it, never on top of it.

A record, however, is only a sensor — a thermometer, not a thermostat. Reading who has harmed and quietly declining to deal with them is not self-government; it is sorting, and sorting taken to its limit is its own failure mode: the cage of norms, coercion by collective exclusion, an in-group reading the marks and freezing out the marked with no appeal. What closes the loop into governance is contestation. A claim of harm must be answerable. In NTARI's design a negative rating is party-initiated and scoped to harm, and it opens a dispute the rated party can contest; an adjudicator can dismiss an unfair claim.

But an adjudicator who can dismiss a claim holds a sovereign function — they decide what counts as harm — and so three further commitments are load-bearing. The claim must run both ways: a producer's rating of a consumer must be as disputable as the reverse, or the system has silently encoded that one class is trustworthy and the other is the risk. The raw rating event must be immutable, so that a dismissal *annotates* rather than erases — a front end may forgive a harm but must never be able to hide one, and a dismissal is itself recorded as a dismissal, visible to anyone downstream. And a pattern of harm must have a path to changing a *rule*, not merely to avoiding a *person*, because avoidance leaves structural power untouched: you can avoid a bad actor, but you cannot avoid a bad matching algorithm.

These commitments are not posture; they are mechanized in **Mycelium**, the ledger that holds the record. Mycelium is append-only and hash-chained, and its unit is not the isolated event but the whole **dialog** — one exchange, with its messages, its transaction, its ratings, and any audit gathered in the order they occurred. A dialog is sealed only once it is complete and no longer contested, and its hash is linked to the hash before it, so that altering any sealed record breaks the chain from that point onward. There is no edit and no delete. A dismissal is therefore not a deletion but a later record that annotates the earlier one; a front end can forgive a harm, but it cannot make one vanish, and the forgiveness is itself on the record. The seal waits on the contest rather than racing it: a claim of harm, in either direction, holds the dialog open until it has been answered, and a party who simply falls silent is, at the close of the rating window, written down as satisfied rather than permitted to stall the account indefinitely.

Identity at this layer is pseudonymous. The written narrative that justifies a claim of harm is operational data held under privacy-first commitments, with any personally identifying information kept local to the front end and never published into the commons. "Data sovereignty" here means the right to carry your record, answer it, and annotate it — not the right to delete another party's attestation about a shared exchange. The record is portable and contestable; it is never erasable.

What the chain commits is the *shape* of the exchange and never its private content. The narrative that justifies a harm — and any datum that could identify a person — does not enter the ledger at all; it is referenced rather than recorded, held local to the front end, and read only by the exchange's own parties and the adjudicator who must weigh it. The immutable record is therefore, by construction, free of personal data. This is the reconciliation the first version never reached between a permanent record and a right to be forgotten: the layer that must never be erased carries nothing personal to erase, and the layer that is personal lives where erasure is possible. Immutability and privacy stop being opponents and become a division of labor.

### 3.2 The frontend layer: plural faces, cheap exit

Voice governs the peer layer. It does not govern the relationship between a community and the software that coordinates it, because a participant cannot litigate the design of a platform the way they litigate a single bad exchange. There the mechanism is exit — but a kind of exit the first version did not have.

NTARI's applications are released under the GNU Affero General Public License, and a single protocol can bind many independent front ends, each with its own administrators, its own interface, its own local culture. The decisive choice is that the things which make exit ruinous — the market itself, the matching and liquidity and counterparties, and the reputation record — live in the *protocol*, not the front end. Leaving a front end therefore costs you the front end and nothing else. You keep the market, and you keep your record. This is the cheap exit that forking a whole network never was.

Cheap exit turns a front end's reputation into a price signal. Administrators are themselves rated, and here — unlike at the peer layer — competence is a genuine aggregate, where a single furious false rating is noise rather than buried harm; the same statistical move that is catastrophic for a harm event is correct for an adjudicator's record. A community can therefore read whether a front end is *improving or declining* and leave on the trend, before it bottoms. Reading the trajectory rather than the level is the verb made visible — the running of the race, observed. It is reflexive: a visible decline accelerates departure, a run. But the run is survivable here, and only here, because the liquidity sits in the protocol; a collapsing front end migrates its users without destroying the market. Front-end mortality is not catastrophe but the system breathing. A bank run is safe exactly where exit is cheap, and nowhere else.

### 3.3 The core: the layer with no exit

Everything cheap to leave lives in the front end. The one thing a community cannot cheaply leave is the protocol, because forking the protocol splits the liquidity. That is secession, not voice, and the right to it — which the license guarantees — is worth nothing without the capacity to exercise it.

It is worth separating the implementation from the specification. The implementation is cheaply forkable: anyone can reimplement the wire format and remain on the network, the way a dozen mail servers all speak the same protocol, and that portability is a real check on whoever runs the reference instance. The specification is not. To fork the spec incompatibly is the schism; whoever governs how the spec evolves governs whether a reimplementation stays on the network at all. The spec is the irreducible constitution — the one layer with no exit, because it is the layer that defines exit for everything beneath it.

That specification is now written rather than merely implied. The Agrinet protocol is set down as a grammar of transmissions in which a message out of order is a message out of protocol, and the Mycelium record it governs is specified beside it. Writing the bottom layer down is what converts it from de-facto code — whatever the reference instance happens to do — into a legible, forkable, contestable constitution a reimplementation can build against. It is worth being exact about what this secures and what it does not: the ordered, signed, sealed record makes the *sequence and content* of an exchange tamper-evident, so that no step can be skipped, reordered, injected, or rewritten after the fact. It does not make the *computation inside a step* honest — an operator can still emit a well-formed, correctly-ordered record of a rigged match. That is the kitchen the recipe cannot open by itself; against it the architecture falls back, as ever, on legibility and the cheapness of exit.

This is where the despotic Leviathan actually hides — not in *having* a conductor, but in pretending the bottom layer is a mesh when it is not. The honest design names the conductor at the spec layer and then does three things to it. It keeps the score minimal: the spec guarantees the portabilities that keep every layer above it leaveable — immutable reputation events, cross-front-end matching and liquidity, portable records — and almost nothing else, so that capturing the spec yields little, because the power lives in the layers that can be left. It keeps the spec legible, so the weak exit stays a real one. And it subjects the spec to standing contestation rather than one-time ratification, because the core is the single layer where the Red Queen's absence of a finish line is literally true: no one can leave it, so it is the one place where the contest must run forever.

The core is also the one place where the organizational culture that the rest of the system leaves emergent must, by contrast, be deliberately structured — not because pre-codifying culture is good in general (that is the static-artifact error again), but because the core is the one place where leaving governance emergent leaves power unchecked. A deliberation procedure at the core, in particular, must resist the most seductive form of the conductor: the synthesis that "honors the range of perspectives" and so averages the outvoted-but-correct dissenter into a consensus. Averaging opinion is the same error as averaging a rating, committed at the level of deliberation; the lone dissenter who is right is exactly the signal a centroid destroys. The core therefore needs a standing-opposition track — a way for a minority position to persist and be re-litigated without first having to prove harm — or its deliberation will regress to the mean and call the result consent.

## 4. Method: Clean-Room Development and the Limits of the Right to Fork

The applications themselves are produced by clean-room reverse engineering. One team documents the observable behavior of an existing coordination platform; a separate team, never seeing the original code, builds a new implementation from those specifications alone; and the result is a legally independent work to which the multi-role, transparent, governable architecture the original lacked can be added. Released under AGPL, it becomes a permanent commons — modifications to a networked service must be shared, incorporation requires opening the whole, and the code cannot be enclosed.

But the license must be understood for precisely what it does and does not secure. It guarantees the *right* to fork; it does not supply the *capacity*. A right to fork a live network is hollow when forking means abandoning the liquidity. The capacity the license cannot grant is supplied instead by two things the architecture must provide: liquidity and reputation that live in the protocol, so the relevant exit — leaving a front end — is cheap; and legibility, so the irreducible exit that remains expensive — reimplementing the spec — is at least possible in practice. The single non-negotiable, then, is that every artifact ships legible: documented and interpretively accessible enough that a downstream community can actually exercise the freedom the license promises. A commons no one can read is a freedom no one can use. For Agrinet that legibility is concrete rather than promised: the wire protocol and the Mycelium record are written specifications, so a downstream community reimplements from a stated constitution, not from a reading of whatever code happens to be running.

## 5. The Steward: Naming the Conductor

A protocol governed by "the community" in the abstract is governed by no one, which in practice means captured by whoever shows up — the tyranny of structurelessness, the cage of norms in open-source clothing. The honest alternative is to name the body that stewards the core.

NTARI is a 501(c)(3) nonprofit, and its bylaws place the protocol's stewardship inside a nested-circle structure modeled on sociocracy. The offices of the corporation are open channels in which any member in good standing deliberates and votes, and each channel elects a recallable representative who serves as its officer and director. This is the reconciliation a legal nonprofit requires: the law vests ultimate fiduciary authority in a board and will not let it abdicate, so rather than pretend the board away, the design fills its seats from the bottom with delegates the channels can recall. Voting is one person, one vote, regardless of contribution tier. And the commitments that matter most — that all protocols remain open-source, that participation is privacy-first, that the system never monetizes surveillance — are entrenched behind a double supermajority, hard to enter and hard to remove. Legibility, the one flag that survives every layer of this argument, is thereby written into the constitution itself.

Honesty requires naming the limit as plainly as the structure. At this core layer, and only here, the one-actor claim does not yet hold. The people governed by the protocol — everyone who transacts on it — are not the same as the people who govern NTARI, who are its members; and membership is presently purchased. The protocols are free to all, but the franchise that steers them is not, and the body that steers them sets the price of the franchise. This is the corridor's hardest seat: the layer with no exit is governed by a class the governed must pay to join, and it binds most heavily on exactly the participants — those who come to a coordination platform from crisis, from reentry, from the need to trust a stranger with a child — whom a Leveson-derived system exists to protect. The commitment to make every participant a member in time is real, but it depends on coordination infrastructure not yet built.

Until then, the gap is named rather than performed. NTARI is, for now, a small, legible, mission-locked steward, disciplined by an entrenched commitment to its own commons, by a fork that is expensive but real, and by an internal contest it is obligated to keep running. The work at the core is not to perfect a governance that cannot be made exit-safe. It is to keep the contested prize small, the exit real, and the opposition standing.

## 6. Research Agenda

The questions that matter for this version are no longer whether transparency is achievable but whether the homeostatic loop actually runs. Does cheap front-end exit materialize in practice, or does liquidity quietly recentralize until leaving costs the market after all? Does contestable reputation metabolize friction into trust, or does it sort a community into mutually avoidant fragments? Do participants read trajectory and leave on the trend, or does an engaged minority govern the core as the iron law of oligarchy predicts? Does an immutable, contestable, non-erasable reputation record survive contact with privacy law and lived expectation — now that the record is built to commit only the shape of an exchange and to keep every personal detail in the erasable, front-end-local layer? And does standing opposition at the core in fact keep the contest running — or does it decay, as every achieved symmetry decays, the moment attention lapses? These are measurements of a practice, not proofs of an architecture.

## 7. Conclusion: Liberty as the Running

The first version's instinct was right: platform power lives in architecture, not ownership. Its error was to stop at the first architectural layer — the presentational one — and to mistake the lighting of the stage for the staging of the play. Community orchestration software is the architecture of the performance: not the transparent screen but the live medium in which coordinating power and checking power keep contending, fast enough and legibly enough that the balance is held by the motion. It collapses Leviathan and society into one actor wherever the governed can become the governing — at the peer layer through voice, at the front-end layer through exit — and it is honest enough to admit that at the one layer no one can leave, the collapse cannot hold, and the most that can be built is a small, named, permanently contested conductor.

The narrow corridor is not a destination the architecture reaches. It is a race the software lets a community run in real time. Liberty at platform scale is not a state that is achieved and then inhabited. It is a contest that is sustained — or it is lost.
